# What Nobody Tells You Before Taking a 24-Hour Cybersecurity Exam

At first, 24 hours sounds like a lot.

You hear:

> **"You have almost an entire day to complete the exam."**

And you think:

*"Perfect. I'll have plenty of time."*

Then the exam starts.

You enumerate the first target.

You find something interesting.

You investigate it.

Nothing.

You try another approach.

Still nothing.

You look at the clock.

It's 3 AM.

And somehow, you've been staring at the same service for two hours.

Now you're not even sure if you're going in the right direction.

Welcome to the part of practical cybersecurity exams that nobody really explains.

The technical skills matter.

But sometimes, **how you manage yourself matters just as much.**

## 24 Hours Does Not Feel Like 24 Hours

This is probably the first thing you should understand.

A long exam window doesn't mean you have unlimited time.

You still need to:

- Enumerate targets
- Identify attack paths
- Exploit vulnerabilities
- Escalate privileges
- Move through the environment
- Document your work
- Troubleshoot failures
- Manage your energy

And the clock keeps moving while you're doing all of it.

The first few hours can feel comfortable.

You are fresh.

You're focused.

Everything feels under control.

Then several hours pass.

Your brain slows down.

Simple things start taking longer.

And suddenly that command you have used a hundred times somehow looks unfamiliar.

That's why time management should be part of your preparation.

# The Biggest Trap: The Rabbit Hole

This is where practical exams become interesting.

You find something.

It looks promising.

You investigate.

Nothing.

But instead of moving on, you think:

> "There has to be something here."

So you keep going.

Another 30 minutes.

Another attempt.

Another tool.

Another Google search.

And suddenly you've spent two hours on one idea.

This is a **rabbit hole**.

And almost everyone falls into one eventually.

The important skill is recognizing when you're in one.

If something isn't producing useful information after repeated attempts, step back.

Take notes.

Move to another target.

Return later with a fresh perspective.

Sometimes the best move isn't another exploit.

It's simply **looking at the problem differently.**

---

# Your First Approach Will Probably Fail

This is another thing beginners often underestimate.

You will probably try something that doesn't work.

That's normal.

Your exploit fails.

Your credentials don't work.

Your shell behaves strangely.

Your privilege escalation path goes nowhere.

A service looks vulnerable but isn't useful.

None of these things automatically mean you're doing badly.

A practical exam isn't designed around everything working perfectly.

You are being tested on your ability to adapt.

Instead of thinking:

> "This didn't work, I'm stuck."

Try thinking:

> **"Okay. That path didn't work. What else does the evidence tell me?"**

That small change in mindset can make a huge difference.

---

# The Clock Changes Your Decision-Making

When you're practicing in a lab, you can spend four hours solving one machine.

During an exam, that decision has consequences.

If you spend three hours chasing one possibility, those three hours are gone.

This is why you need a rough strategy before the exam begins.

For example:

**Initial phase:**  
Enumerate everything you can.

**Next phase:**  
Identify the most promising attack surfaces.

**Middle phase:**  
Exploit and escalate.

**Later phase:**  
Revisit unresolved targets.

**Final phase:**  
Focus heavily on documentation and verification.

The exact strategy will depend on the certification and exam structure.

The important part is having **a strategy instead of improvising for the entire exam.**

# Don't Forget That You Still Have to Write a Report

This one hurts.

You spend hours getting access.

You finally reach the objective.

You feel relieved.

Then you remember:

> **"I still need to document everything."**

And now you are trying to remember which command you ran three hours ago.

Don't do this to yourself.

Keep your notes organized while you work.

Save:

- Commands
- Important output
- Screenshots
- Credentials discovered during the engagement
- Attack paths
- Privilege escalation steps
- Evidence of impact

Professional reporting is part of penetration testing.

Don't treat it as something that happens after the "real" work.

The report **is part of the work.**

# Sleep and Breaks Are Not a Waste of Time

This might sound strange during a long exam.

But taking a break can actually save time.

If you've been staring at the same problem for two hours and you're exhausted, another hour probably won't magically make you smarter.

Step away.

Drink water.

Eat something.

Walk around.

Clear your head.

Come back.

You may return and immediately notice something you completely missed before.

You are not competing to see who can stay awake the longest.

You're trying to solve problems effectively.

# The 3 AM Problem Is Real

There is a psychological point during long exams where confidence can suddenly disappear.

At 10 PM:

> "I've got this."

At 1 AM:

> "Still fine."

At 3 AM:

> "Maybe I should have become a graphic designer."

😂

This is normal.

Fatigue makes problems feel bigger than they actually are.

Don't make major decisions about your ability at 3 AM.

If you're tired, take a break.

If you're stuck, change the target.

If you're frustrated, step away for a few minutes.

Your exam performance is not a measurement of your worth.

It is one assessment on one day.

---

# What Should You Practice Before the Exam?

Don't only practice technical exploitation.

Practice the **exam experience**.

Try doing labs with a timer.

Practice taking structured notes.

Practice writing findings immediately after discovering them.

Practice moving away from a machine when you're stuck.

Practice returning to an old target later.

And most importantly:

> **Practice solving problems without immediately opening a walkthrough.**

Walkthroughs are excellent learning tools.

But if you always use one the moment you get stuck, you never develop the ability to sit with a problem and work through it.

That ability matters enormously during practical exams.

---

# A Simple Mindset That Helps

Before starting a long practical exam, remember these five things:

**You will get stuck.**

That's normal.

**Some approaches will fail.**

That's normal too.

**You will probably miss something at some point.**

Go back and enumerate again.

**You will feel tired.**

Take breaks.

And finally:

**You do not need to solve everything at once.**

One target.

One clue.

One step.

Then the next.

# Final Thought

A 24-hour cybersecurity exam isn't really about surviving for 24 hours.

It's about making good decisions while your energy, time, and patience are slowly being tested.

You will get stuck.

You will question your approach.

You will probably have at least one moment where you think:

> **"There is absolutely no way I'm solving this."**

Then you take a breath.

You enumerate again.

You notice one small detail.

And suddenly, everything starts making sense.

That's the feeling you're preparing for.

So don't just prepare to **hack the machines**.

Prepare to manage yourself.

Because sometimes the biggest challenge in a long cybersecurity exam isn't the target sitting in front of you.

**It's the person sitting behind the keyboard.**
