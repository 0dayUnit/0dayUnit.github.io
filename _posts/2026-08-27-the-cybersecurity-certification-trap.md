# The Cybersecurity Certification Trap: Why You Shouldn't Collect Certifications Randomly

It usually starts innocently.

> "I want to get into cybersecurity."

Then you discover certifications.

**Security+.**

Okay, that sounds useful.

Then someone tells you about **CEH**.

Then you hear about **eJPT**.

Then **CPTS**.

Then **OSCP**.

Then someone mentions **PNPT**.

And suddenly your plan looks like this:

> Security+ → CEH → eJPT → CPTS → PNPT → OSCP → OSEP → CRTP → CRTE → ...

At some point, you stop learning cybersecurity and start collecting certifications.

Congratulations.

You've become a certification collector. 😂

But here's the problem.

**More certifications do not automatically mean more skills.**

Sometimes, taking fewer certifications with a clear purpose can take you much further.

## The Problem With Collecting Certifications

There is nothing wrong with certifications.

A good certification can:

- Give you structured learning
- Build practical skills
- Validate your knowledge
- Strengthen your resume
- Help you prepare for a specific role

The problem starts when the certification becomes the goal instead of the **skill**.

You finish one exam and immediately ask:

> "What's next?"

Not:

> "What did I actually learn?"

That difference matters.

Imagine spending six months collecting three certificates but still struggling to perform a basic penetration test without a walkthrough.

You have three certificates.

But the skill gap is still there.

---

# Stop Asking "What's the Best Certification?"

This is probably the most common question in cybersecurity.

> "What's the best cybersecurity certification?"

There isn't one.

The better question is:

> **"What's the best certification for my current goal?"**

Because a beginner learning networking has completely different needs from someone preparing for a red team role.

A web application pentester has different priorities from an Active Directory specialist.

And someone trying to move into security operations probably shouldn't follow the exact same certification roadmap as a penetration tester.

Your destination should decide your roadmap.

Not someone else's LinkedIn profile.

---

# If You Want to Become a Red Teamer

If your goal is **red teaming**, don't build your roadmap around random certifications.

Start by building strong penetration testing fundamentals.

Then move toward areas such as:

- Active Directory
- Windows environments
- Internal network attacks
- Privilege escalation
- Lateral movement
- Command and control
- Evasion concepts
- Red team operations

Certifications such as **OSCP, CRTP, CRTE, OSEP, and CRTO** can fit into this kind of journey depending on your current skill level and specific goals.

But you don't need to take all of them back-to-back.

Learn.

Practice.

Then choose the next certification based on the skill you actually need.

---

# If Web Pentesting Is Your Goal

Your roadmap should look different.

Instead of collecting general cybersecurity certifications, focus heavily on:

- HTTP fundamentals
- Authentication
- Authorization
- APIs
- SQL injection
- XSS
- SSRF
- XXE
- Business logic
- Web exploitation
- Source code review

Certifications such as **CBBH, eWPT, eWPTX, and OSWE** can make more sense depending on your experience level.

The important part is not the number of certificates.

It's whether each step is making you a better web pentester.

---

# If Active Directory Is Your Thing

This is another area where people often take the wrong approach.

You don't necessarily need five general pentesting certifications before learning Active Directory.

If enterprise environments and AD attacks are your goal, you can deliberately build skills around:

- LDAP
- Kerberos
- Windows authentication
- Domain enumeration
- ACL abuse
- Delegation
- Credential attacks
- Privilege escalation
- Lateral movement
- ADCS

Certifications such as **CRTP and CRTE** can fit naturally into an Active Directory-focused path.

The point isn't to collect both because they exist.

The point is to understand **why you are taking them**.

---

# What If You're Completely New?

This is where the certification trap can become expensive.

If you're a beginner, you don't need to immediately chase the hardest certification you can find.

Build your foundation first.

Learn:

- Networking
- Linux
- Windows
- Basic scripting
- Web fundamentals
- Security concepts
- Enumeration
- Basic exploitation

Then start working with practical labs.

A certification such as **eJPT** can be a reasonable starting point for someone building practical penetration-testing fundamentals.

From there, you can decide whether you want to move toward CPTS, PNPT, OSCP, web security, Active Directory, or another specialization.

You don't have to know your entire cybersecurity career path on day one.

# The "One More Certification" Problem

This is where things get dangerous.

You finish a certification.

You feel good.

Then you see someone post another one.

Suddenly you think:

> "Maybe I need that too."

So you start studying again.

Then another certification appears.

And another.

Before you realize it, you've spent months preparing for exams without spending enough time actually building projects, solving labs, or understanding real-world environments.

There's always another certification.

You will never reach the end of the list.

And that's okay.

Because **the goal was never to finish the list.**

---

# A Better Way to Choose Your Next Certification

Before paying for another exam, ask yourself five questions:

### 1. What role am I targeting?

Pentester?

Red teamer?

Web security?

AD specialist?

Security analyst?

Know your direction.

### 2. What skills am I missing?

Don't choose a certification just because it is popular.

Choose one that fills a gap.

### 3. Am I ready for it?

A difficult certification taken too early can waste both time and money.

### 4. Will this certification actually help my goal?

If you cannot explain why you're taking it, stop and think.

### 5. What will I do after passing?

This is the question people forget.

A certificate should be a step toward something.

Not the final destination.

---

# You Don't Need to Follow Someone Else's Roadmap

This is probably the biggest lesson.

You will see people with:

**10 certifications.**

**20 certifications.**

**A wall full of badges.**

Good for them.

But your journey doesn't need to look like theirs.

Maybe your first certification takes six months.

Maybe you fail once.

Maybe you change your specialization.

Maybe you discover that web security interests you far more than network pentesting.

That's normal.

Cybersecurity is a huge field.

Give yourself permission to explore.
