# Is OSCP Still Worth It in 2026?

Let's start with the question almost every aspiring penetration tester eventually asks.

> **Is OSCP still worth it?**

And usually, that question comes right before another one.

> **"Because this thing is expensive... so I really don't want to make a stupid decision."**

Fair.

Very fair.

For years, OSCP has had a certain reputation in cybersecurity.

You hear about it when you start looking seriously at penetration testing.

Someone mentions it in a job requirement.

Someone posts that famous certification PDF on LinkedIn.

Someone tells you:

> "Bro, do oscp and change the game."

And suddenly, you're thinking about it too.

Then you look at the price.

You look at the course.

You look at the labs.

You look at the exam.

And then you look at your bank account.

Now the question becomes a little more serious.

> **Is OSCP actually worth all of this in 2026?**

Not just the money.

The time.

The frustration.

The weekends.

The machines that somehow refuse to work even though you're *sure* you're doing everything correctly.

And yes...

The emotional damage.

Just an honest look at what OSCP means in 2026, who should consider it, and who probably shouldn't.

# First, What Is OSCP in 2026?

OSCP is one of the most recognized practical penetration testing certifications from OffSec.

The current certification is associated with the **OSCP+** designation and the PEN-200 learning path.

The exam is practical and designed around performing penetration testing activities inside a controlled exam environment.



You actually have to work.

You have to enumerate.

You have to identify weaknesses.

You have to exploit them.

And you have to document what you did.

At some point, you have to sit in front of the screen and make decisions.

What should I enumerate next?

Did I miss something?

Is this actually a vulnerability or am I wasting two hours?

Should I keep going?

Or should I move on?

That decision-making process is a huge part of penetration testing.

And honestly, that's one of the reasons OSCP still has value.

But Let's Talk About the Elephant in the Room: The Cost
=======================================================

This is where the conversation gets real.

OSCP is not a ₹2,000 course that you casually buy on a Friday night because you suddenly felt motivated after watching a hacker movie.

And that is exactly why the question matters.

You're not only asking:

> **"Can I pass OSCP?"**

You're also asking:

> **"Should I spend this much money on OSCP?"**

Those are two completely different questions.

Because yes, you _can_ eventually pass it.

But that doesn't automatically mean it's the right investment for you **right now**.

The Biggest Mistake: Buying OSCP Because Everyone Else Is Doing It
==================================================================

This happens more often than people admit.

Someone starts learning cybersecurity.

They watch a few YouTube videos.

They solve a few easy machines.

Then they join Discord.

And suddenly, everyone is talking about:

*   OSCP
    
*   CPTS
    
*   PNPT
    
*   CRTP
    
*   OSEP
    

And before they even understand what they actually enjoy doing, they're planning a certification roadmap until 2030.

Slow down.

You do **not** need to collect certifications like Pokémon.

The first question should be:

> **What do I actually want to become good at?**

If your goal is penetration testing, then yes, OSCP might make sense.

If you're primarily interested in web application security, you may need a different path.

If you're deeply interested in Active Directory and enterprise environments, there are other certifications that may give you more specialized depth.

If you're still struggling with basic Linux commands, networking, or enumeration...

Maybe spending thousands on OSCP isn't your next move.

And there is absolutely nothing wrong with that.

Starting earlier doesn't always mean finishing earlier.

Sometimes it just means getting frustrated earlier.

Is OSCP Difficult?
==================

Short answer?

**Yes.**

But probably not for the reason many people think.

The technical knowledge matters, obviously.

You need to understand enumeration.

You need to know Linux and Windows.

You need to understand privilege escalation.

You need to approach Active Directory with a methodology.

You need to know how to troubleshoot.

But one of the hardest parts is something else.

Knowing When to Stop.
---------------------

Imagine this.

You find a service.

You see something interesting.

You start testing it.

Thirty minutes pass.

Nothing.

One hour.

Still nothing.

Now you're convinced:

> "This HAS to be the path."

Two more hours disappear.

Congratulations.

You may have just entered a rabbit hole.

The OSCP journey is not only about finding vulnerabilities.

It's also about managing your own decisions.

The ability to recognize when something is going nowhere is a skill.

And trust me, that skill becomes useful far beyond an exam.

So... Is OSCP Still Respected?
==============================

Yes.

But let's be realistic about what that means.

OSCP still has strong name recognition in the cybersecurity industry, especially within penetration testing and offensive security roles.

That recognition is valuable.

When a recruiter, hiring manager, or security professional sees OSCP on a resume, they generally understand what type of certification it is.

It signals that the candidate has gone through a practical offensive security assessment.

But here's something important.

> **OSCP is not a job guarantee.**

Passing OSCP doesn't automatically mean:

What About CPTS, PNPT, CRTP and Other Alternatives?
===================================================

This is probably where the OSCP discussion becomes interesting.

Because in 2026, OSCP is no longer the only name people consider.

You have options.

For example:

*   **CPTS** offers a structured learning path with extensive practical content.
    
*   **PNPT** focuses heavily on practical penetration testing methodology.
    
*   **CRTP** focuses on Active Directory attack techniques.
    
*   **eJPT** can be a more approachable starting point for beginners.
    
*   **OSCP** remains one of the best-known names in practical penetration testing.
    

So which one is better?

Honestly?

That's the wrong question.

The better question is:

> **Better for whom?**

Someone completely new to penetration testing may benefit more from building fundamentals before attempting OSCP.

Someone specifically interested in Active Directory may get more immediate value from an AD-focused path.

Someone looking for broad industry recognition may still see OSCP as an important goal.

There is no universal certification that magically fits everyone.

And anyone telling you:

> **"This is the only certification you need."**

is probably oversimplifying the problem.

Should a Beginner Take OSCP?
============================

I'm going to say something that might save someone a lot of money.

**Not every beginner needs to start with OSCP.**

That doesn't mean beginners are incapable of passing it.

It means preparation matters.

If you currently struggle with:

*   Basic networking
    
*   Linux fundamentals
    
*   Windows administration
    
*   Enumeration
    
*   Web fundamentals
    
*   Privilege escalation
    
*   Active Directory concepts
    

then OSCP can become unnecessarily overwhelming.

Build the foundation first.

Practice.

Break things.

Get stuck.

Learn how to get unstuck.

Then move forward.

The OSCP journey is difficult enough already.

There is no prize for intentionally starting unprepared.

The Part Nobody Really Likes Talking About: Failure
===================================================

Let's be honest.

You can prepare for months and still fail.

That possibility is real.

And when you're spending serious money, failing feels different.

You don't just think:

> "I'll try again."

You think:

> "Okay... that hurt."

But failing an exam doesn't automatically mean you're bad at cybersecurity.

Sometimes it means:

*   Your methodology broke down.
    
*   You spent too much time on one machine.
    
*   You panicked.
    
*   You missed something obvious.
    
*   Your preparation had gaps.
    
*   You were simply not ready yet.
    

That's why I think people should stop treating certification failure like some kind of career-ending event.

It isn't.

Painful?

Absolutely.

Embarrassing?

Only if you decide it is.

The important question after a failed attempt is:

> **What did this exam teach me that I didn't know before?**

Because if you can answer that honestly, the attempt wasn't completely wasted.

What Has Changed With OSCP?
===========================

The OSCP exam has evolved over time.

That means one important thing for anyone preparing today:

**Don't blindly follow old advice.**

Cybersecurity changes.

Exams change.

Course content changes.

You might find an old YouTube video.

An old Reddit post.

A checklist from three years ago.

And assume everything still works exactly the same way.

Always verify important details using the latest official documentation before scheduling your attempt.

Your preparation should be based on the current exam requirements, not someone's 2019 blog post.

Is OSCP+ a Problem?
===================

Another thing candidates need to understand is the newer **OSCP+** designation.

Before purchasing or preparing for the exam, make sure you understand the current certification structure and any maintenance requirements that apply.

This is one of those details people often ignore until after they pass.

Which is understandable.

You're busy trying to root machines.

Reading certification policies isn't exactly exciting.

But it's still worth understanding before you spend the money.

Okay, But Is It Actually Worth the Money?
=========================================

Now we're back to the original question.

And my honest answer is:

**For the right person, yes.**
------------------------------

OSCP can be worth the investment if:

*   You genuinely want to work in penetration testing or offensive security.
    
*   You already have a reasonable technical foundation.
    
*   You want a practical certification with strong industry recognition.
    
*   You're willing to spend serious time practicing.
    
*   You're not expecting the certification alone to get you a job.
    
*   You understand that the learning process matters more than just the PDF at the end.
    

But I would think twice if:

*   You're only doing it because someone on LinkedIn told you to.
    
*   You're expecting an automatic salary increase.
    
*   You haven't built basic technical foundations yet.
    
*   The cost would put you under serious financial pressure.
    
*   You're more interested in another cybersecurity specialization.
    
*   Your only goal is adding four letters to your LinkedIn headline.
    

There's nothing wrong with deciding:

> **"Not yet."**

In fact, sometimes "not yet" is a much smarter decision than "let's see what happens."

My Honest Verdict
=================

If someone asked me:

> **"Should I do OSCP in 2026?"**

I wouldn't immediately say yes.

I'd ask them a few questions first.

What do you already know?

What role are you targeting?

How comfortable are you with Linux and Windows?

Can you enumerate without immediately reaching for a walkthrough?

Have you spent enough time inside labs?

Can you troubleshoot when the obvious path doesn't work?

And probably the most important question:

> **Are you doing this because you want the skills, or because you want the badge?**

Because if it's only about the badge, OSCP is going to feel very expensive.

But if you're genuinely trying to become a better penetration tester, the journey itself can be valuable.

The certification becomes proof of a process you already went through.

Not the entire purpose of it.
